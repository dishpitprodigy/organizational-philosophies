# Author Interview Brief

This is an editorial source-gathering instrument, not reader-facing manuscript copy. Its purpose is to produce two related pieces without turning either into a narrated résumé:

1. a short **About the Author** grounded in verifiable professional history; and
2. a first-person **Preface** explaining why this particular history led to this particular book.

## Evidence to assemble before the interview

- Current and historical CVs or résumés
- Cover letters and recruiter-facing professional summaries
- Certification records and formal training history
- Existing descriptions of significant projects
- Public biographies or speaker profiles, if any
- Dates and role names needed to distinguish memory from documented chronology

The record establishes what happened. The interview establishes what it meant.

## Interview questions

### The practical lineage

1. What is the earliest example you remember of noticing waste, motion, ambiguity, or poor flow before you had formal language for it?
2. What did the automotive warehouse teach you that you immediately carried into information work?
3. At BP, which concrete projects changed how you thought about service operations, measurement, and organizational decision-making?
4. What did formal Six Sigma training clarify that practice alone had not?
5. What did ITIL give you that Lean and Six Sigma did not, and where did ITIL become ceremony rather than control?
6. What did the Agile transformation at ADP teach you from the perspective of IT Operations and as someone helping deliver the training?

### The synthesis

7. Which parts of Lean, Six Sigma, ITIL, Agile, and SRE are describing the same underlying mechanism in different vocabularies?
8. Where do those methods genuinely disagree or operate at different levels?
9. Which principles are non-negotiable, and which implementations should always be adapted to local conditions?
10. When did you realize project execution methods were insufficient without an organizational evidence and decision system around them?
11. What recurring organizational failure connects work intake, project design, operations, talent development, and vendor selection in your mind?

### The evidence behind the argument

12. Which three projects best demonstrate the method rather than merely credentialing you?
13. For each project, what was the initial condition, what evidence changed the diagnosis, what intervention followed, and what result or unresolved question remained?
14. Where were you wrong, and what evidence forced you to update your model?
15. Which ideas in the book were learned from successful systems, and which were reverse-engineered from preventable failures?
16. Which claims should be presented as direct experience, which as inference, and which still need external research?

### The authorial position

17. Who is the reader you most want to stop and think, “I have been accepting something I should have classified as a defect”?
18. What do you want a reader to be able to implement on Monday without asking you what “choose relevant metrics” means?
19. What do you believe after roughly two decades of applying these ideas that a newly certified practitioner is unlikely to understand yet?
20. Why did this book become necessary now?
21. What would make the book feel dishonest, overly polished, or unlike you?
22. Which parts of your biography are relevant to the authority of the argument, and which would merely be résumé ornament?

## Proposed outputs

The **About the Author** should be approximately 250–400 words, third person, factual, and selective. It should establish relevant experience without claiming that credentials prove the argument.

The **Preface** should be approximately 1,000–1,800 words, first person, and organized around the development of the governing idea rather than a year-by-year career chronology. It may use one or two compact project examples, but the full worked cases belong in the chapters where they teach a method.

Before publication, the author should review a fact table containing every employer, role, date, certification, project, and measurable claim used in either piece.
